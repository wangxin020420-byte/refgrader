a b s t r a c t
Reducing decision risk is a core objective for decision-makers. Three-way decision offers a novel 
technique for risk reduction through its “accept-reject-defer” framework. However, existing meth­
ods generally rely on subjectively set constant thresholds and loss weights, limiting the application 
scope and effectiveness. To address this, this paper proposes a threshold-functionalized three-way 
decision framework, allowing thresholds to adaptively change with model uncertainty to achieve 
a robust balance between risk control and predictive performance. First, a model pool comprising 
seven classifiers and three probability calibration methods is constructed. The model demonstrat­
ing the highest stability and probability reliability on the validation set is selected to generate 
calibrated probabilities for risk-based decision-making. Subsequently, model uncertainty 𝑢 based 
on calibrated probabilities is introduced, and fixed thresholds are extended into monotonic and 
globally disjoint threshold functions 𝛼(𝑢) and 𝛽(𝑢), enabling the model to adaptively divide three-
way regions. Furthermore, an adaptive threshold function learning method is proposed. It utilizes 
class-conditional quantiles to determine reasonable threshold intervals and applies Winsorization 
and Bootstrap shrinkage estimation to ensure stable threshold variations across uncertainty ranges, 
avoiding oscillations caused by data sparsity or noise. Finally, the optimal threshold shape satis­
fying risk constraints is searched within multiple function families. Experiments on rheumatoid 
arthritis data and supplementary UCI datasets validate the generalizability and effectiveness of this 
method. This study provides a practical technical approach for three-way decision, demonstrating 
its unique advantages and strong application potential.
1 . Introduction
With the proliferation of large-scale complex data and high-risk application scenarios, making decisions that are interpretable, 
quantifiable, and controllable in uncertain environments has become a long-standing challenge. Traditional binary decisions can only 
make rigid judgments between “accept” and “reject,” struggling to capture the intermediate states widely present in reality, such as 
“postpone,” “conduct further observation,” or “submit for expert review.” Three-way decision (TWD), grounded in rough set theory, 
emerged to address this gap. Building upon Pawlak’s rough sets—specifically their positive, boundary, and negative regions—Yao 
interpreted these as three distinct actions: “accept,” “defer,” and “reject.” This formed the systematic TWD framework alongside 
the trisecting–acting–outcome (TAO) model [1–3]. In decision-theoretic rough sets (DTRS), decision-makers derive thresholds by

applied in medical diagnosis, credit risk assessment, recommendation systems, anomaly detection, and other domains [5–8], becoming 
a vital tool for risk-informed decision-making under uncertainty.
Regarding how to characterize and utilize loss (or utility) information, three-way decision-related research has broadly converged 
on two technical approaches. One category of work is based on real-valued loss functions. For instance, Deng and Yao employed 
Shannon entropy to measure uncertainty, incorporating an information-theoretic interpretation of thresholds [
9]; Liu et al. constructed 
discriminant models using logistic regression or its variants, deriving thresholds through probabilistic estimation and cost functions 
[
10]. The other category employs fuzzy or intuitionistic fuzzy loss functions, using linguistic intuitionistic fuzzy numbers, interval-
valued intuitionistic fuzzy numbers, and type-2 fuzzy sets to characterize experts’ subjective evaluations of different actions, thereby 
deriving three-way decision rules [
11]. These studies have greatly enriched the representational forms of loss functions in three-way 
decision. However, a common issue is that the loss parameters themselves often still require manual specification or semi-manual 
adjustment. Their rationality and transferability depend on expert knowledge, making subjectivity difficult to avoid.
To further mitigate this subjectivity, a series of studies has attempted to establish connections between three-way decision and 
behavioral decision theory. Zhu et al. introduced regret theory into three-way decision, estimating conditional probabilities through 
dominance relations to construct regret-based utility functions for determining three-way decision thresholds [
12]. Huang et al. further 
integrated prospect–regret theory with intuitionistic fuzzy numbers within incomplete multi-scale decision information systems [13]. 
These works reshape loss functions or utility functions from psychological and behavioral decision-theoretic perspectives, theoretically 
aligning more closely with human decision processes. However, new parameters—such as loss intervals, value function specifications, 
and risk attitude coefficients—still require prior specification. This approach does not fundamentally eliminate subjectivity but merely 
shifts it from loss values to other parameter domains.
Parallel to loss modeling, multi-granulation rough sets and multi-granulation three-way decisions have evolved. Qian et al. 
proposed a multi-granulation approach, synthesizing decision conclusions from different granulation levels [14,15]. Recently, a 
multi-granulation three-way decision model based on weighted probability kernels provided a unified computational framework 
for data-driven loss functions in hypertension diagnosis by integrating multi-kernel probability similarity with grey relational anal­
ysis. It also employs knowledge-consistency measures to adaptively determine granulation weights [
16]. This work demonstrates 
that three-way decision mechanisms can form richer representations and reasoning capabilities in multi-granulation, multi-source 
information, and multi-perspective environments. However, in most models, thresholds still appear as scalar quantities, typically set 
empirically or optimized once via a fixed objective (e.g., minimizing loss).
In a more refined branch of threshold research, scholars began directly treating the threshold itself as an optimization target. 
Liu et al. defined a loss function using interval-valued intuitionistic fuzzy sets in DTRS, proposing an optimization-based three-way 
decision method to reduce the computational overhead of threshold determination [17]; Shen et al. constructed a single-objective 
function based on information gain, selecting the threshold pair that maximizes information gain [18]. Li and Shao, addressing 
criminal association identification problems, combined uncertainty in the boundary region (measured by entropy) with the overall 
three-way decision cost as trade-off objectives. They automatically searched for thresholds using methods like particle swarm opti­
mization under a given loss matrix [
19]. Bisht and Pal recently proposed the 3WD–MADM model, which simultaneously considers 
loss and utility. It derives a set of thresholds from both expected loss and expected utility sides, then obtains the final threshold via a 
weighted averaging operator [
20]. Liang and Yang conducted an in-depth analysis of this model, pointing out that directly weighted 
thresholds may conflict with the principle of maximum expected utility. They proposed a modification approach based on the nec­
essary conditions for Bayesian decision-making [
21]. These works advance the direction of “optimizable and learnable” thresholds 
from different angles, yet significant limitations remain: First, thresholds are often treated as global scalars, failing to capture contin­
uous variations in uncertainty levels across samples. Second, most objective functions consider only overall cost or boundary region 
size, rarely explicitly incorporating safety constraints like the high-risk leak rate. Third, the shape of thresholds (e.g., monotonically 
increasing or nonlinearly varying with uncertainty) lacks systematic characterization, resulting in limited interpretability.
Another closely related line of research focuses on incomplete/hybrid data, boundary region processing, and adaptive feature 
granularity. In incomplete hybrid data scenarios, prior work has proposed a three-way decision model based on random forest 
imputation, attention mechanism attribute assessment, and multi-T-norm dynamic fusion. This model constructs cost functions from 
both holistic and incremental perspectives and provides an adaptive threshold pair setting strategy using a descending approach. 
Regarding boundary region handling, Li et al. progressively narrowed boundary regions through tri-training combined with rough set 
and centroid methods, enhancing performance in text classification and binary classification tasks [
22,23]; Ma and Yao emphasized 
the importance of negative rules in boundary processing, proposing multiple category-specific three-way reduction forms based 
on category-specific reduction [
24]; Chen et al. proposed a multi-view, cost-sensitive three-way decision model to extract global 
decision information from POS/NEG samples for re-decision on BND samples [25,26]. The AH3 model, building upon MinCA-based 
three-way partitioning, constructs hierarchical feature spaces on positive and negative domains respectively. It selects the optimal 
feature representation through BND validation and adaptive granularity decomposition for secondary three-way decision on boundary 
samples [
27]. These studies reveal that within both sample and feature spaces, researchers predominantly focus on reducing boundary 
region scale through granularity adjustment and multi-stage processing, while threshold designs largely remain confined to “discrete 
alternatives + global constants.”
In summary, existing decision research on threshold modeling exhibits significant gaps and contradictions: on one hand, loss 
functions have been endowed with rich theoretical semantics (fuzzy, behavioral decision-making, multi-criteria, grey correlation, etc.), 
and various attempts at “threshold optimization” and “adaptive selection” have emerged; on the other hand, thresholds are ultimately

Based on the above analysis, this paper addresses the challenges of three-way decision thresholds characterized by strong sub­
jectivity and difficult-to-capture shapes. It proposes a threshold functionalization and parameter adaptation framework centered on 
model uncertainty. First, a model pool comprising seven classifier types and three probability calibration methods is constructed. 
A well-calibrated probabilistic prediction model is selected, and a model uncertainty metric is derived to quantify the “difficulty of 
classification” for each sample. Second, we automatically determine threshold endpoints and variation ranges based on this met­
ric. To mitigate threshold fluctuations caused by data sparsity and noise, robust statistical techniques including Winsorization and 
Bootstrap shrinkage estimation are explicitly incorporated into this parameter generation process. This generates a family of thresh­
old function candidates satisfying monotonicity and non-intersection constraints, enabling continuous adjustment of the three-way 
decision thresholds in response to model uncertainty. Finally, we comprehensively evaluate threshold candidates using metrics such 
as coverage rate (the proportion of samples where the model directly provides explicit “accept/reject” judgments), boundary rate, 
high-risk leak rate, error rate, over-treatment rate, and predefined safety constraints. The Pareto knee point between coverage and 
high-risk leak rate automatically determines the operating point. This mechanism is applied to both “low/high-risk classification” 
and hierarchical segmentation within risk tiers, forming a two-stage three-way decision process.
Compared with existing studies, the main novelties of this paper can be summarized in the following three aspects:
1. A model-uncertainty-driven threshold functionalization method for three-way decisions is proposed. Existing studies on 
three-way decisions mostly use global fixed thresholds (𝛼, 𝛽) to partition the acceptance region, rejection region, and boundary 
region, and the threshold form is usually static, making it difficult to fully adapt to the variation in uncertainty levels among 
different samples. To address this issue, this paper extends the traditional constant thresholds to threshold functions 𝛼(𝑢) and 
𝛽(𝑢) that vary continuously with model uncertainty, so that the three-region partition can be adaptively adjusted according 
to sample uncertainty. This method is not a simple replacement of the traditional threshold form, but promotes three-way 
decisions from “fixed-threshold-driven” to “function-driven under uncertainty conditions” at the decision-mechanism level, 
thereby enhancing the expressive ability of the model for complex sample distributions. Meanwhile, basic shape constraints 
such as monotonicity and non-crossing are imposed to ensure that the threshold functions have clear, stable, and interpretable 
decision semantics.
2. A risk-constrained adaptive learning mechanism for threshold parameters is constructed. In traditional three-way deci­
sion methods, the threshold determination process often relies heavily on prior loss matrices, manually specified experience, 
or fixed rules, and is therefore easily affected by subjective factors in complex tasks. To this end, this paper starts from the 
calibrated predicted probabilities and their uncertainty distributions, and adaptively determines the endpoint intervals, varia­
tion amplitudes, and candidate shapes of the threshold functions, while completing parameter screening and operating-point 
determination under safety constraints such as the high-risk missed-detection rate. This mechanism enables the construction 
of threshold functions to be jointly driven by data distribution, risk objectives, and coverage requirements, thereby reduc­
ing dependence on repeated manual tuning and strong prior loss specification. Compared with existing static-threshold or 
experience-based setting methods, the parameter learning method proposed in this paper has greater advantages in objectivity, 
transferability, and reproducibility.
3. An interpretable three-way decision modeling framework for high-risk applications is established. This paper incorpo­
rates probability model selection, probability calibration, threshold functionalization, and Pareto knee-point selection into a 
unified procedure, and realizes closed-loop modeling from probability output generation to three-region partition decision. It 
should be pointed out that the model pool, calibrators, and multi-metric evaluation mainly serve as supporting mechanisms 
in this paper, providing more reliable probabilistic inputs and unified comparison criteria for threshold-function learning; the 
core methodological novelty of this paper lies in the threshold functionalization and its adaptive learning mechanism itself. 
Experimental results on real medical grading data and multiple public datasets show that the proposed framework performs well 
in the trade-off between coverage and high-risk missed-detection rate, boundary-region control, and decision interpretability, 
indicating that it can not only preserve the risk-control semantics of three-way decisions, but also possesses good engineering 
implementability, thus providing a feasible and extensible technical path for the application of three-way decision methods in 
high-risk scenarios.

2 . Preliminaries
Three-way decision is an extension of the traditional two-way decision framework. Its core idea is that, in addition to the two 
conclusions “accept” and “reject,” a third action “defer” (or “hesitate”) is introduced, so that samples can be divided into the positive 
region, boundary region, and negative region. Decision-theoretic three-way decisions are generally constructed upon the framework 
of decision-theoretic rough sets (decision-theoretic rough sets, DTRS), where loss functions and Bayesian risk provide the foundation 
for deriving three-way decision rules and threshold values.
Definition 2.1.In DTRS theory, for a given decision problem, each sample has two possible true states, denoted as Ω = { 𝑋,¬ 𝑋}, 
where 𝑋 indicates that the sample actually belongs to the target class (or “positive class”), and ¬𝑋 indicates that the sample does not 
belong to the target class (or “negative class”). For the same sample, the decision maker may choose among three different decision 
actions, denoted as {𝛼𝑃 , 𝛼𝐵, 𝛼𝑁 }, where:
• 𝛼𝑃 : positive decision, usually corresponding to assigning the sample to the positive region;
• 𝛼𝐵: boundary decision, corresponding to assigning the sample to the boundary region;
• 𝛼𝑁: negative decision, corresponding to assigning the sample to the negative region.
For any combination of true state and decision action (𝜔, 𝛼), DTRS characterizes the resulting cost through a loss function. A 
commonly used form is the following loss table:

the thresholds determined by the loss function divide the conditional probability interval into three segments, thereby forming the 
positive region, boundary region, and negative region—constitutes the foundation of Decision-Theoretic Three-Way Decisions.
3 . Methodology for constructing model uncertainty based on model ensemble and probability calibration
This section aims to convert the outputs of various candidate classification models into credible and interpretable calibrated 
probabilities, and subsequently construct a model uncertainty measure based on these probabilities, providing a unified and stable 
input for the threshold functionalization and three-way decision in later sections. Specifically, after uniform preprocessing, this 
paper first constructs a model pool comprising multiple probabilistic classifiers and three types of probability calibrators. Out-of-fold 
predictions are obtained through stratified 𝐾-fold cross-validation, and probability calibration is performed on each “model-calibrator 
combination” to yield the sample-level calibrated probability ̃ 𝑝(𝐱). Subsequently, based on the out-of-fold calibrated probabilities, 
this paper systematically compares evaluation metrics such as NLL, ECE, Brier, and AUC. Following the priority order of “NLL first, 
then ECE, then Brier, and finally AUC,” the best-performing model and calibrator combination is automatically selected, and the 
unified calibrated probability 
𝑝(𝐱) is provided accordingly. Building upon this, this paper further converts the calibrated probability 
into a model uncertainty characterization 𝑢(𝐱), ultimately outputting {𝑝(𝐱), 𝑢(𝐱)} and the corresponding calibration evaluation results, 
laying the foundation for subsequent model uncertainty-driven threshold function learning.

The model pool constructed in this paper includes seven representative supervised classification algorithms: LightGBM [28], 
Logistic Regression [29], Random Forest [30], Gradient Boosting Decision Tree [31], Support Vector Machine [32], Multi-Layer 
Perceptron [33], and Naïve Bayes [34]. These models cover various underlying preferences such as linear models, tree-based models, 
ensemble models, and neural networks. By characterizing the data distribution from different structural assumptions and functional 
forms, they provide a more comprehensive and robust source of reference for probability calibration and uncertainty measure­
ment. The model pool is expandable, and in practical applications, other classifiers can be flexibly added or removed based on 
task complexity and data characteristics.
It should be noted that the candidate classifiers in the model pool are not exhaustively tuned in a classifier-specific manner for 
each dataset. Instead, commonly used empirical settings in standard implementations are adopted as initial operating points, together 
with light pilot adjustments to exclude obviously unsuitable configurations. This design is motivated by three considerations. First, it 
helps ensure a fair comparison across heterogeneous classifiers and avoids introducing additional bias by tuning one particular model 
more aggressively than others. Second, it reduces manual intervention and improves the reproducibility of the overall pipeline. Third, 
it enhances the transferability and applicability of the framework to unseen datasets. On this basis, different candidate classifiers are 
further combined with multiple calibrators and compared under a unified set of probability-quality criteria, so that the more suitable 
classifier–calibrator combination can be adaptively determined for the current task. In this way, the model pool remains extensible 
while providing stable and comparable probabilistic inputs for subsequent threshold-function learning.

3.2.3 . Best classifier–calibrator combination selection
This study selects Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Brier Score, and Area Under the ROC Curve 
(AUC) as four types of metrics to evaluate the “model-calibrator” combinations. The reason is that these four metrics measure the 
discriminative ability, calibration degree, and overall error level of the probability output from different angles, possessing strong 
complementarity. Specifically, NLL reflects the overall quality of the probability distribution fit and is the most commonly used 
log-likelihood type metric in current probabilistic model training and selection; ECE characterizes the bin-wise deviation between 
predicted probability and true frequency, serving as the most intuitive metric for measuring the degree of probability calibration; Brier 
Score synthesizes accuracy and calibration in the sense of mean squared error, providing an overall characterization of probability 
error; AUC characterizes the probability ranking ability, used to measure the model’s discrimination effect on high-risk samples. 
These four types of metrics constitute a relatively complete probability quality evaluation system from the four aspects of likelihood, 
calibration, mean squared error, and ranking performance. This provides a stable and reliable basis for selecting the optimal “model-
calibrator” combination and ensures that the subsequently constructed uncertainty measures and threshold functions have good 
statistical significance and decision interpretability.

4 . Model uncertainty-driven three-way decision threshold functionalization modeling and parameter adaptive 
optimization framework
In traditional three-way decision, the division of acceptance, rejection, and deferment regions relies on manually set fixed thresh­
olds. The entire decision boundary remains unchanged during the testing phase, unable to automatically adjust according to the 
reliability of the model’s judgment on samples. This static threshold often lacks flexibility when dealing with samples that are diffi­
cult to judge, easily leading to situations such as “high-risk samples being accepted too early” or “low-risk samples being unnecessarily 
deferred,” thereby limiting the performance of three-way decision in risk-sensitive tasks. To overcome this limitation, this paper gen­
eralizes fixed thresholds into functions that automatically vary with model prediction reliability, enabling decision boundaries to 
adaptively contract or expand based on judgment difficulty, thereby achieving more reasonable and robust risk control. This section 
unfolds around the design of threshold functionalization modeling and adaptive learning methods centered on this core idea.
Specifically, based on the calibrated probability ̃ 𝑝 and model uncertainty 𝑢 obtained in Section 3, this section generalizes three-
way decision thresholds from constants to functionalized boundaries {𝛼(𝑢), 𝛽(𝑢)}. We model the threshold functions into three types 
of interpretable function families (Linear, Power, and Logistic). On the validation set, we calculate metrics such as safety (high-risk 
leakage rate) and efficiency (Coverage rate) for each candidate. We first apply safety hard constraints, then construct a safety-efficiency 
frontier on the feasible solution set, and automatically select the operating point using the Knee Point principle. By comparing the 
knee point performance across families, we ultimately select the optimal family and its parameters. This process does not require 
manually specifying (𝛼, 𝛽), realizing the adaptive determination of function families and thresholds.
4.1 . Three-way decision threshold functionalization
After obtaining the calibrated prediction probabilities and the corresponding uncertainty measures, the goal of this subsection is to 
extend the fixed thresholds in three-way decision-making into functional forms that vary continuously with the level of uncertainty. To 
this end, we first need to formally describe the threshold division method of three-way decision-making under uncertainty measures 
and further specify the basic properties that threshold functions should satisfy. The formal definition of three-way decision threshold 
functionalization is given below.

4.2 . Adaptive learning of threshold functions
4.2.1 . Adaptive selection of candidate parameters for threshold functions
After determining the structure of the threshold function families, the next step is to determine the feasible parameter ranges 
for these functions, so that they satisfy the safety constraints of three-way decision while achieving adaptive adjustment based 
on data distribution. To this end, starting from the model uncertainty distribution in the validation set, this subsection constructs 
the candidate parameter set for threshold functions through two mechanisms: “Endpoint Anchoring” and “Amplitude Constraint.” 
Endpoint anchoring is used to determine the starting position of the function in the low-uncertainty region, ensuring it matches the 
class distribution of real data; amplitude constraint limits the maximum extension range of the threshold function over the entire 
uncertainty interval, avoiding excessive bending or instability caused by sample fluctuations. Combining the two forms a set of robust 
candidate parameter intervals that satisfy basic constraints, providing a search space for the subsequent Pareto optimal selection.

4.2.2 . Optimal selection of threshold functions and parameters based on adaptive learning
In constructing the model uncertainty-based threshold functionalization thre
e-way decision model, merely providing the form 
of threshold functions is insufficient to complete the design of the overall decision mechanism. To ensure the model works stably 
under different risk exposure scenarios, two key questions must be answered simultaneously: (i) How to evaluate the three-way 
decision output structure (POS/NEG/BND) induced by the threshold functions; (ii) Under strict safety constraints, how to select the 
optimal scheme from numerous feasible threshold functions. Therefore, this section first constructs a set of evaluation metrics capable 
of reflecting risk sensitivity for the three-way decision output, and on this basis, proposes a “Safety First” oriented multi-objective 
threshold function selection framework to systematically complete the feasibility determination and optimal solution selection of 
threshold functions.
(1) Adaptive Evaluation Metrics
In practical applications of three-way decision, different types of decision errors have significant asymmetric costs: if high-risk 
samples are misjudged as safe classes, it usually brings losses far higher than the misjudgment of low-risk samples, while excessive 
conservatism (frequently entering the deferment region) increases manual review costs and system latency. Therefore, a set of evalu­
ation metrics capable of simultaneously characterizing the “safety, efficiency, and cost trade-off” is needed to comprehensively reflect 
the performance of three-way decision under different risk exposure scenarios.
Based on these practical needs, starting from the output structure (POS/NEG/BND) of three-way decision, this paper selects five 
categories of metrics: Coverage, BND Ratio, Decision Error Rate, High-Risk Leakage Rate, and Over-Treatment Rate. Among them, 
Coverage and High-Risk Leakage Rate constitute the two core quantities that best reflect the objectives of risk-sensitive tasks: Coverage 
measures the system’s ability to automatically make definite decisions, while High-Risk Leakage Rate directly reflects the most critical 
“whether high-risk samples are correctly identified” under safety constraints. The remaining three metrics (BND Ratio, Decision Error 
Rate, Over-Treatment Rate) are used to reveal the behavioral bias and cost structure of the model in different operating intervals 
(POS, NEG, BND), providing auxiliary interpretation for understanding model performance.

(2) Multi-objective Threshold Function Selection under Safety Constraints
In the optimization of threshold functions for three-way decision, the model output must first satisfy the “Safety First” principle of 
risk-sensitive tasks, i.e., the High-Risk Leakage Rate (Leak) must not exceed the maximum tolerance level allowed by the domain. On 
this basis, Coverage (Cov) is used to measure the extent to which the system can automatically make definite decisions while ensuring 
safety conditions; whereas the over-treatment rate (Over) reflects the system’s additional costs and resource occupancy. Therefore, 
the selection of threshold functions naturally forms a “Safety Hard Constraint + Multi-objective Trade-off” problem structure, where 
safety is a prerequisite constraint, and efficiency metrics (such as coverage and error rate) seek reasonable improvement within the 
feasible range, rather than blindly pursuing extreme values.
In this context, this section constructs a unified Feasible Region—Dominance Relation—Knee Point Selection framework to sys­
tematically screen the optimal threshold function family from all candidate parameters. The logic of this framework is clear: (1) 
First, the feasible solution set is determined by safety constraints; (2) Then, dominance relations are used in the feasible region to 
eliminate inferior solutions; (3) Finally, the optimal trade-off solution between efficiency and safety is selected through the Knee 
Point principle.
Definition 4.4  (Safety Feasible Region).Given a high-risk leakage tolerance level 𝜏∈ [0,1]  and an optional over-processing upper 
bound 𝜗∈ [0,1] , the safety feasible region of the candidate parameter set 𝐶 is defined as: 
F𝜏,𝜗 = {ℎ∈𝐶∶Leak(ℎ)≤𝜏,Over(ℎ)≤𝜗}.(28)
where ℎ represents a set of threshold function parameters. The feasible region ensures that all subsequent optimizations are performed 
under conditions that satisfy basic safety requirements; if the Over constraint is not set, let 𝜗= +∞ .
Here, 𝜏 and 𝜗 are not threshold parameters that directly determine the three-region partition, but feasibility constraints used to 
characterize the safety requirements of the application scenario. Specifically, 𝜏 controls the tolerable upper bound of high-risk missed 
diagnoses, and 𝜗 controls the acceptable range of overtreatment. These two quantities are usually specified according to the safety 
requirements and risk preferences of the specific task; when overtreatment is not emphasized in the application, 𝜗 can be relaxed. 
Therefore, what is reduced in this paper is the dependence on subjective specification of fixed thresholds in three-way decisions, 
rather than completely eliminating all safety constraints with explicit application semantics.
Definition 4.5  (Safety Dominance).For any ℎ1, ℎ2 ∈F 𝜏,𝜗, if Leak(ℎ2)≤Leak(ℎ 1) and Cov(ℎ2)≥Cov(ℎ 1), and the inequality holds 
strictly on at least one side, then ℎ2 is said to dominate ℎ1. All solutions not dominated by any other scheme form the Pareto Frontier 
set P⊆F 𝜏,𝜗.
In the Pareto frontier, the lower the high-risk leakage rate, the higher the coverage is ideal, but there is usually an inherent 
contradiction between the two that cannot be optimized simultaneously. To achieve a stable trade-off between the two, the Knee 
Point principle is adopted to select the optimal solution.
Definition 4.6  (Knee Point).Let 𝐶 be the given set of threshold function parameter candidates, F𝜏,𝜗 ⊆ 𝐶  be the feasible region

frontier as Leakmin = min ℎ∈P Leak(ℎ) and Covmax = max ℎ∈P Cov(ℎ). Normalize the two objectives as:

5 . Experimental verification based on rheumatoid arthritis diagnosis data
In this section, based on the model uncertainty-based adaptive threshold three-way decision model proposed in the previous text, 
we construct a three-way decision diagnosis scheme for rheumatoid arthritis. Specifically, we first abstract the clinical diagnosis 
process of rheumatoid arthritis into a discrimination problem with multiple test indicators, then use real clinical data to construct a 
decision information system, and on this basis apply the proposed threshold functionalization and parameter adaptive method. We 
carried out systematic experiments on 14,280 actual examination records to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the model’s diag­
nostic performance and risk control ability, and compared it with several baseline methods to verify the effectiveness and superiority 
of the proposed method. In addition, to verify the generalization ability and robustness of the method, this chapter further repeats 
the experimental process on an external independent dataset, further demonstrating the effectiveness of the model.
5.1 . Rheumatoid arthritis description
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized mainly by chronic synovitis, which can involve multi­
ple joints and lead to joint destruction and functional impairment, and is one of the leading causes of long-term disability and reduced 
quality of life. The onset of RA is mostly insidious, the disease course is prolonged, and clinical manifestations include joint pain, 
swelling, morning stiffness, and a series of systemic symptoms. Its pathogenesis involves multiple factors such as genetic susceptibility, 
environmental factors, and immune abnormalities. The diagnosis and assessment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) need to combine clin­
ical manifestations and laboratory/imaging indicators, mainly including: Rheumatoid Factor (RF), anti-Cyclic Citrullinated Peptide 
Antibody (Anti-CCP Antibody), C-Reactive Protein (CRP), Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR), and joint imaging examination in­
dicators, etc. From the perspective of data analysis, the diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis can be regarded as a discrimination problem 
based on multi-indicator information, suitable for research using three-way decision models capable of explicitly characterizing model 
uncertainty and decision costs.

5.3 . Experimental methodology design
The experiments in this section revolve around the model uncertainty-driven three-way decision threshold functionalization frame­
work proposed in Section 3 and the function families and parameter adaptive strategies presented in Section 4. The overall process 
adopts the philosophy of “Hierarchical Diagnosis + Stratified Threshold Learning”: First, the four-level labels 0–3 of rheumatoid 
arthritis are compressed into two categories: “Low Risk (0/1)” and “High Risk (2/3),” constructing a binary classification model for
Stage-1 to distinguish between the overall high and low-risk populations; on this basis, fine-grained three-way decision structures 
for Stage-2 are formed by distinguishing “0 vs. 1” within the low-risk group and “2 vs. 3” within the high-risk group (
Fig. 2).
In specific implementation, the experiment first cleans and preprocesses the raw data, removing identity information fields, 
and performing median imputation and missing indicator expansion for missing values. Subsequently, winsorization truncation is 
performed on continuous variables to weaken the impact of extreme values on model training and probability estimation. Based on the 
preprocessed data, a model pool containing LightGBM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), 
SVM, MLP, and Naïve Bayes is constructed for each stage. Through the three-step process of “Training–Calibration–Validation,” the 
optimal “Classifier + Probability Calibrator” combination is selected: a portion of the calibration set is first partitioned within the 
training set to calibrate candidate models using Isotonic Regression, Platt Sigmoid, and Temperature Scaling; then, metrics including 
NLL, ECE, Brier Score, and AUC are calculated on the independent test set. The model-calibrator combinations are ranked according 
to the priority of “Minimize NLL first, then Minimize ECE and Brier, and finally Maximize AUC,” and the combination with the best 
comprehensive calibration performance is selected as the baseline probability estimator for that stage.
After obtaining the calibrated posterior probability 𝑝(𝑥), the experiment calculates the model uncertainty measure 𝑢(𝑥) = 1 −
|2𝑝(𝑥) − 1| according to the definition in Section 3. Based on the high model uncertainty sub-region in the training set, the endpoint 
grid and slope upper bounds are adaptively constructed to generate three types of threshold function candidate families: Linear 
(affine), Power function (power), and Logistic. For each set of candidate function parameters, the three-way decision evaluation 
criteria from 
Section 4 are used to simultaneously calculate metrics such as Coverage, Boundary Ratio, High-Risk Leakage Rate, and 
Over-Treatment Rate on the test set, screening out the feasible solution set that satisfies the preset high-risk leakage constraints (e.g., 
𝐿𝑒𝑎𝑘≤0.05  in Stage-1). Subsequently, a Pareto frontier is constructed on the “High-Risk Leakage – Coverage” plane, and the final 
threshold function is selected according to the “Closest to Ideal Point” Knee Point principle. The experiment realizes a complete data-
driven pipeline from “Model-Calibrator Combination” to “Model Uncertainty-Driven Threshold Functions,” making the three-way 
decision threshold no longer a manually given global constant, but a functional form automatically learned for specific datasets and 
specific risk requirements. The specific results will be discussed in 
Section 5.4.

5.4.1 . Probability modeling and calibration performance
This paper first evaluates the comprehensive effects of the model pool and the three probability calibration strategies. Table 2 
presents the NLL, ECE, Brier Score, and AUC of the optimal “Model × Calibrator” combination at each stage. It can be seen that in

performance, while in Stage-2 (2 vs 3), LightGBM+ Platt achieved the best performance.
The reliability curve of Stage 1 (Fig. 3(c)) demonstrates good calibration characteristics. Overall, the curve is distributed roughly 
along the diagonal within the entire confidence range. It is slightly higher than the diagonal in the medium confidence interval 
(0.3 − 0 .6) and almost overlaps with the diagonal in the high confidence interval (0.8 and above), with only slight fluctuations 
near 0 caused by a small number of samples. This indicates that on the general binary task of “Low Risk (0/1)” vs. “High Risk 
(2/3),” the model’s estimation of its own confidence is relatively accurate or slightly conservative. In other words, in Stage 1, the 
probability output is credible on a global scale, providing a solid foundation for mapping confidence to model uncertainty measures 
and performing threshold functionalization based on 𝑢.
The reliability curve of Stage 2 (0 vs 1) (Fig. 3(a)) tightly adheres to the diagonal line as a whole. It basically overlaps with the 
ideal line in the medium-to-low confidence interval (0 − 0.6) and is located slightly above the diagonal in the high confidence interval 
(0.6 and above), meaning that the empirical accuracy is generally slightly higher than the confidence given by the model. This shows 
that on the low-risk subset (distinction between 0 and 1), the optimal combination selected by “Model Pool + Three Calibrations” 
not only possesses strong discriminative ability but also presents a slight “conservative” characteristic in probability measurement; 
that is, in the high confidence interval (above 0.8), the predicted probability given by the model is usually lower than its actual hit 
rate. Corroborated by the subsequent three-way decision results, the reliability analysis of this stage indicates that in mild lesion 
recognition scenarios, the probability output by the model can serve as a robust basis for risk quantification.
The reliability curve of Stage 2 (2 vs 3) (Fig. 3(b)) generally rises monotonically along the diagonal. Within most confidence 
intervals, the empirical accuracy is close to the ideal line, indicating that the model’s probability measure for the high-risk internal 
subdivision task is generally credible. In the medium-to-low confidence interval (approximately 0 − 0.6), the curve fluctuates slightly 
up and down, but the deviation amplitude is limited; as the confidence increases to above 0.6, the empirical accuracy improves sig­
nificantly, and it basically fits or is slightly higher than the diagonal in the high confidence segment (0.8 and above), indicating that 
when the model gives high confidence, its prediction results have high reliability. Compared with Stage 1 and Stage 2 (0 vs 1), Stage 2 
(2 vs 3) reflects a finer-grained distinction between Grade 2 and Grade 3. Therefore, moderate fluctuations in the medium-to-low con­
fidence region are reasonable, which also highlights that introducing model uncertainty-driven three-way threshold functionalization 
in this stage can further reduce the risk of wrong decisions by keeping low-confidence samples in the boundary region.

6 . Conclusions and future work
This paper addresses the challenge of dynamically characterizing three-way decision thresholds in high-risk scenarios by proposing 
a model uncertainty-driven threshold functionalization and parameter adaptive learning framework. The core innovation lies in 
breaking the limitations of global constant thresholds in traditional three-way decisions. By integrating probability calibration and 
uncertainty quantification, we construct a family of threshold functions that evolve dynamically with classification difficulty, enabling 
adaptive contraction and expansion of decision boundaries at different uncertainty levels. In the empirical study of rheumatoid 
arthritis grading, the framework significantly narrows the boundary region bandwidth under strict safety constraints and achieves an 
improved trade-off between the error rate of decided samples and coverage. Furthermore, experimental results on 13 heterogeneous 
public datasets demonstrate that the proposed method (TF-3WD) achieves performance comparable to or, in specific metrics, superior 
to representative three-way decision models without requiring a complex sequential structure, further validating the robustness and 
generalizability of the framework.
Although the framework exhibits strong robustness and application potential, its effectiveness in practical engineering imple­
mentation remains contingent upon specific environments. First, as a highly data-driven approach, the performance of the method 
is closely linked to the calibration quality of the underlying model’s output probabilities. In specific domains where samples are 
extremely scarce, the lack of representative probability distributions may pose challenges for the fine-grained adjustment of adaptive 
thresholds. Second, while the existing linear, power, and logistic threshold forms maintain excellent interpretability, there remains 
room for further optimization regarding the fitting of certain complex non-linear relationships in ultra-high-dimensional feature 
spaces. Furthermore, from the perspective of uncertainty modeling depth, the measure 𝑢(𝑥) defined in this paper primarily focuses on 
capturing the aleatoric uncertainty near the classification boundary, which represents the classification difficulty. To identify epis­
temic uncertainty beyond the model’s knowledge boundaries and out-of-distribution (OOD) samples, more complex measures will be 
required in the future to enhance the system’s completeness in open-world medical decision-making.
Future research will focus on three dimensions. First, we will explore deeper uncertainty representation techniques, considering the 
introduction of Monte Carlo Dropout or deep ensemble methods to construct composite measures that characterize both classification 
difficulty and model knowledge boundaries, thereby improving system reliability when facing unknown distributions. Second, more 
flexible function approximators satisfying monotonicity constraints will be introduced to further enhance the expressive precision 
and search efficiency of threshold curves while maintaining clear decision semantics. Third, we aim to deepen research on parameter 
configurations across multiple scenarios, attempting to integrate domain expert knowledge and industry compliance standards to 
incorporate risk constraint parameters and loss matrices into a unified intelligent risk configuration framework. Such developments 
are expected to promote the widespread application of three-way decision theory in more high-risk fields, such as medical grading 
and credit risk control.
